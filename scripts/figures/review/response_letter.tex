\toeditor
We thank the editor and reviewers for their thoughtful comments. The revisions and response address every comment. The main changes are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Independent voxel-space evaluation of text-to-brain maps against simple baselines and existing, published models (\cmt{1.1}).
  \item Retrieval measured by recall@k, mean reciprocal rank, and median rank, also against hard negatives (\cmt{1.1}).
  \item A comparison of generated and retrieved captions (\cmt{3.3}).
  \item A data-flow diagram of the training stages (\cmt{1.2}).
  \item NeuroVault results reported as an upper bound, a lower bound, and a multi-target evaluation, with map types handled separately (\cmt{1.3}).
  \item A leakage audit beyond DOI matching (\cmt{2.4}).
  \item Out-of-distribution and failure-case analyses (\cmt{1.4} and \cmt{2.12}).
  \item Corrected numbers, names, and claims throughout.
\end{itemize}

We also updated the model and its evaluation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The text-to-brain head is trained with a new loss, latent error plus decoded-map correlation, which makes its maps more accurate (\cmt{1.1} and \cmt{3.5}).
  \item Generated maps are calibrated to the scale of the training maps, with one factor per model fitted on the validation set.
  \item An MLP projector replaces the Qformer in both brain-to-text stages (\cmt{2.1}).
  \item The language model still generates every title, steered toward the canonical network and region names by masking tokens that cannot continue one (\cmt{2.12}).
  \item The synthetic training maps had included held-out papers. We rebuilt them from training papers only and retrained the models that use them (\cmt{2.4}).
  \item Every evaluation leaves out the duplicated papers found by the leakage audit (\cmt{2.4}).
  \item All result figures were redrawn from the final model and optimized for ease of viewing.
\end{itemize}

\reviewer{1}
\revcomment{1.0}{General remarks}\begin{revquote}
This is an ambitious and timely manuscript introducing \textbf{NeuroVLM}, a unified framework for contrastive text–brain retrieval, text-to-brain generation, and brain-to-text generation. The integration of these capabilities within a modular architecture is appealing, and the use of a relatively large corpus of 30,826 publication-level text–neuroimage pairs is a notable strength. The public release of the code, pretrained models, and datasets should also be valuable to the community. The results provide encouraging evidence that the model learns meaningful associations between scientific text and coarse spatial brain representations. However, I think several central aspects of the evaluation and data construction require further clarification or analysis before the stronger claims regarding map generation and neuroimage interpretation are fully supported.
\end{revquote}\response We added the requested analyses and clarifications (\cmt{1.1} to \cmt{1.10}).

\revcomment{1.1}{More independent evaluation of the generative outputs}\begin{revquote}
\textbf{1. More independent evaluation of the generative outputs is needed}
\revqpar The current generative evaluation is performed partly within NeuroVLM’s own learned representation space. Generated maps and text are embedded using the model’s contrastive projection heads and then ranked against candidate items in that same space. This is a useful measure of internal consistency, but it does not independently establish that a generated map is anatomically accurate or that a generated description faithfully reflects the source image.
\revqpar The matched-versus-null results are statistically strong, although some absolute differences are relatively modest—for example, Dice is 0.47 versus 0.40, BERTScore is 0.56 versus 0.52, and semantic cosine is 0.47 versus 0.40. These findings support above-null alignment, but additional validation would help establish neuroscientific specificity.
\revqpar I encourage the authors to add some combination of:
\begin{itemize}
\item Evaluation using independent image and text representations not used during NeuroVLM training.
\item Blinded expert ratings of anatomical accuracy, semantic accuracy, specificity, and unsupported claims.
\item Hard negatives drawn from the same cognitive domain or from studies with similar spatial patterns.
\item Comparison between generated outputs and their nearest training examples.
\item Strong simple baselines, including nearest-neighbor retrieval, mean or domain-specific maps, linear regression from text embeddings to spatial coefficients, and atlas-based labeling.
\end{itemize}
\revqpar It would also be useful to report conventional retrieval measures such as recall@1, recall@5, recall@10, mean reciprocal rank, and median rank, in addition to the area under the full recall@k curve.
\end{revquote}\response We agree. Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} and Table~\ref{tab:t2b} score text-to-brain maps in voxel space, against each test paper's reconstructed map. NeuroVLM leads, narrowly ahead of its linear-head version and ahead of Text2Brain, mean and domain maps, atlas labeling, Neurosynth, NeuroQuery, and the nearest training paper (Dice 0.53 against at most 0.49). Neurosynth, NeuroQuery, and Text2Brain are used as published, although their corpora each hold nearly half the test papers.

\textbf{Independent evaluation.} Both directions are also scored outside NeuroVLM's representation space. Maps are compared in voxel space, and NeuroVLM also leads against the raw coordinate maps. MiniLM cosine and BERTScore, whose encoders were not used in training, compare generated captions with the paper's text (Reviewer 3, \cmt{3.3}).

\textbf{Matched and shuffled pairs.} BERTScore separates matched from shuffled pairs only weakly (0.553 against 0.548) with the null computed from the same captions. BERTScore mostly rewards the style of the reference, an LLM summary, rather than the domain-specific correctness. Random training texts in that style score higher (Table~\ref{tab:genret}).

\textbf{Modest differences.} The narrow gaps in BERT and semantic scores are a result of using general models without fine-tuned neuroscience domain knowledge. The in-domain gap between matched and null distributions is larger. The evaluation within NeuroVLM's latent space is on the left-out test set and shows that domain-specific encoders are required to evaluate performance.

\textbf{Expert ratings.} We did not run blinded expert ratings, because they are time consuming and hard to scale. Instead, we used existing expert labels and automatic checks. For anatomical and semantic accuracy, region titles are checked against anatomical atlases and network titles against expert atlas labels (Reviewer 3, \cmt{3.6}). Hard negatives test specificity. Unsupported claims are counted as study details that a map cannot support (Reviewer 3, \cmt{3.3}).

\textbf{Hard negatives.} Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} scores retrieval among all test papers and among hard negatives, the test papers of the query's own cognitive domain. Hard negatives are only slightly harder than random papers of the same number (recall@10 0.24 against 0.28).

\textbf{Nearest training examples.} Generated maps match each test paper's map better than the map of the nearest training paper does (Table~\ref{tab:t2b}). By MiniLM cosine, generated captions are as close to the paper's text as the nearest training caption. However, 57\% of these retrieved captions report details of another study, against 2\% of generated captions. Reviewer 3 raised the same concern (\cmt{3.3}).

\textbf{Simple baselines.} Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} and Table~\ref{tab:t2b} include each suggested baseline. The linear regression is NeuroVLM with a linear head. It maps the SPECTER2 embedding to the autoencoder latent, the spatial coefficients of each map. We fit it by ridge regression, with the penalty chosen on the validation set. The non-linear head adds a hidden layer and replaces cross-entropy with a new loss, latent error plus decoded-map correlation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\textbf{Retrieval measures.} Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} reports recall@1, recall@5, recall@10, mean reciprocal rank, and median rank in both directions. Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} and Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} are new in the Supplementary Information.

\revcomment{1.2}{Publication-level supervision and dataset separation}\begin{revquote}
\textbf{2. The publication-level supervision and separation of datasets across training stages need clarification}
\revqpar The primary training pairs associate publication titles and abstracts with maps reconstructed from reported coordinate tables. Because a single neuroimaging publication may include multiple experiments, contrasts, populations, and positive or negative effects, the correspondence between an abstract and one aggregated map may be substantially many-to-many.
\revqpar Please clarify:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Whether coordinates were combined across all experiments and contrasts within each publication.
\item How positive and negative effects were handled.
\item How multiple coordinate tables associated with one DOI were treated.
\item The final numbers of publications, experiments, contrasts, and coordinate tables.
\item Whether a contrast-level evaluation can be provided for a subset of the data.
\end{enumerate}
\revqpar The complete train–test separation across model components also needs to be documented more clearly. In particular, Qwen3-0.6B was adapted using 1.2 million neuroscience articles. Please confirm that held-out PubMed and NeuroVault publications were excluded from this language-model corpus. Similarly, the manuscript should explain how test concepts and documents were excluded when constructing the synthetic fine-tuning data and canonical prototype bank. The synthetic stage uses generated maps and descriptions of common networks, regions, and cognitive concepts, creating another possible route through which test information could enter the system.
\revqpar A data-flow diagram showing the datasets used for autoencoder training, contrastive training, language-model adaptation, Qformer training, synthetic fine-tuning, validation, and final testing would substantially improve transparency.
\end{revquote}\response Each map pools all coordinates of one paper. We added the requested data-flow diagram and the final counts.

\textbf{1. Experiments and contrasts.} Each paper's map combines the coordinates of all its experiments and contrasts. The released coordinates contain no experiment or contrast labels. Each map matches the paper-level text it is paired with.

\textbf{2. Positive and negative effects.} Positive and negative effects are pooled, because the released coordinates do not contain signs. Each map therefore only shows where effects lie. The Methods state this.

\textbf{3. Multiple coordinate tables.} All coordinate tables of a paper are pooled into one map. Among open-access papers with a coordinate table, 42\% report two or more.

\textbf{4. Final counts.} Table~\ref{tab:counts} gives the final counts, 30,826 publications with 857,298 peaks.

\textbf{5. Contrast-level evaluation.} A contrast-level evaluation is possible only on NeuroVault, whose maps are released per contrast (\cmt{1.3}). We added a multi-target evaluation, where each study's text is paired to multiple reported maps. With every map as its own target, the model beats chance in both text-to-brain and brain-to-text directions (AUC 0.62 and 0.63).

\textbf{Separation of datasets.} Fig.~\ref{fig:dataflow} is the requested data-flow diagram. No validation or test paper trains the autoencoder, projection heads, or projectors. The language model's training script removes the validation, test, and NeuroVault evaluation papers from its corpus. Generated captions share only short phrases with their abstracts (median two words).

\textbf{Synthetic data and prototype bank.} The synthetic maps use training papers only (Reviewer 2, \cmt{2.4}). The prototype bank holds network, region, and function texts, not test documents. It names the networks that label the evaluation atlas maps, but no atlas network map trains any component.

Providing ablation results in the supplementary materials could compare the importance of different modeling decisions (such as the cross-entropy loss and canonical projection matrix).
\end{revquote}\response The shared latent dimensions are interpretable, word swaps move the generated map but negation does not, and the cross-entropy loss matters most for retrieval.

\textbf{1. Encoder weights.} We interpret the latent dimensions that texts and maps share, rather than raw weights. For the clearest dimensions, the decoded map matches the Neurosynth map of the dimension's top term, such as listening in superior temporal cortex (Fig.~\ref{fig:latent}, $r = 0.65$). Text and maps agree on what each dimension means. We added this figure to the Supplementary Information.

\textbf{2. Perturbations.} NeuroVLM responds to word swaps and to joined concepts (Fig.~\ref{fig:probes}) but ignores negation. Swapping one word moves the map toward the matching Neurosynth contrast, and joining two concepts covers both. A sentence and its negation give nearly the same map ($r = 0.84$ to 1.00). We find no evidence of composition beyond addition. We added this limitation to the Discussion.

\textbf{3. Ablations.} The cross-entropy loss matters most for retrieval. Without it, retrieval AUC drops from 0.83 to 0.62, while the temperature and a one-directional loss barely matter (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The final text-to-brain head also beats heads trained with other losses. Comment \cmt{3.3} gives the effect of the canonical projection on captions. We added these ablations to the Supplementary Information.
